# Stage 4c — Cover letters, application emails, and open questions

The resume is the evidence; these are the argument. They appear at three moments:

| Artefact | When | Length |
|---|---|---|
| Application email | When the employer takes applications by email, or you are following up | 120–180 words |
| Cover letter | When the posting asks for one, or the employer is small enough that a human reads it | 250–400 words |
| Open-question answers | Inside the online form itself ("Why this role?", "Career plan") | 100–250 words each |

All three follow the same logic as the resume: **map to the JD, cite real evidence, do not pad.**

## The rule that separates a good letter from a bad one

A weak letter restates the resume with adjectives:

> I am a hardworking and passionate student with strong communication skills and a great
> ability to learn. I am very interested in this position and believe I am a good fit.

Every sentence here could be pasted into any application for any company. It communicates nothing.

A strong letter answers three questions, in this order:

1. **Why this role?** — one specific thing about the work itself.
2. **Why you?** — two or three pieces of *evidence*, matched to that role's requirements.
3. **Why this employer?** — one specific, verifiable thing about them.

## Structure (cover letter)

```
Paragraph 1  — The hook (2–3 sentences)
               Name the role. State the one-line reason you are a fit.
               Do not open with "I am writing to apply for..."

Paragraph 2  — Evidence A, tied to requirement 1
               Concrete action + scale + result. Mirror the JD's vocabulary.

Paragraph 3  — Evidence B, tied to requirement 2
               Same discipline. A different competency, not a repeat.

Paragraph 4  — Why this employer (2–3 sentences)
               Something specific and verifiable: a practice, a product, a recent
               initiative. Not "your company is a leader in the industry."

Paragraph 5  — Close (1–2 sentences)
               Availability, and a plain thank-you. No grovelling.
```

## Bilingual notes

- Write in the **JD's language**. A Chinese posting gets a Chinese letter; an English posting gets English.
- Do not translate a letter word-for-word between languages — the register differs. A Chinese letter
  can be more formal and use 敬语; an English letter should be plainer and more direct.
- Keep names and titles consistent with the resume in both languages.

## Application emails

Subject line format:

```
<Role title> — <Your name> — <one differentiator>
```

Example: `Data Analyst Intern — {{FULL_NAME}} — Python + 2 years of research data work`

Body: the cover letter, compressed to 120–180 words, with the resume attached as a PDF named
`{{FULL_NAME}}_{{ROLE}}.pdf`. State the attachment in the body so a stripped attachment is obvious.

For a **follow-up**: reply in the original thread, restate the role and application date, add one new
piece of information (a completed certification, an updated project), and keep it under 80 words.
Never follow up more than twice, and never inside the stated decision window.

## Open questions in online forms

These are frequently the *actual* filter, so they deserve more care than the resume upload.

**"Why this role / why this company?"**
Give one role-specific reason and one employer-specific reason. The employer-specific one must be
verifiable — if the interviewer could not confirm it, it does not count.

**"What are your career plans?"**
Show a direction that this role plausibly serves, over a 3–5 year horizon. Avoid both "I want your
job" and a plan that has nothing to do with the employer.

**"Describe a challenge you overcame."**
Use a **real** example in CAR form (Challenge, Action, Result). One paragraph. The lesson at the end
is what makes it land.

**"What is your greatest weakness?"**
Name a genuine, non-fatal one, then the concrete mitigation. "I used to under-document my analysis,
so I now write a short methods note as I go" beats a disguised strength.

**"Anything else we should know?"**
Only if you have something substantive: a portfolio, a relevant side project, a constraint worth
stating (availability, location). Otherwise leave it empty — padding here reads as filler.

## Consistency discipline

Every claim in a letter or an open answer must be **defensible from the resume you submitted**.
Before sending, place the letter beside the resume and the JD snapshot and check:

- [ ] Each factual claim appears on the resume, or is a stated-honestly extra.
- [ ] No new numbers that contradict the resume.
- [ ] Dates and graduation timing match exactly.
- [ ] The employer-specific sentence is accurate and current (re-check the source).
- [ ] The role title matches the posting's exact wording.

## Anti-patterns to cut on sight

- Opening with "I am writing to apply for…"
- "I am a hardworking/passionate/detail-oriented person" with no evidence behind it
- Listing every skill the candidate has, regardless of the JD
- Praising the employer with adjectives ("world-class", "industry-leading") and no fact
- Repeating the resume line by line
- Any sentence that would still be true for a different candidate applying to a different company

The test: **delete the employer's name and the role title — does the letter still make sense?**
If yes, it was generic. Rewrite it.
